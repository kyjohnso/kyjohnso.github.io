🧬 Genetic Inheritance
DNA, genes & chromosomes · dominant & recessive alleles · Punnett squares · carriers · inherited disorders

From cell to gene — zooming in

The nucleus holds chromosomes. Each chromosome is one very long coiled molecule of DNA. A gene (yellow) is a short section of DNA that codes for one characteristic.

📦 DNA, genes, chromosomes & the nucleus

  • The nucleus controls the cell and contains the genetic material.
  • Chromosomes are long threads of coiled DNA found in the nucleus. Human body cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs) — one of each pair from your mother, one from your father.
  • DNA is the chemical that carries the genetic code. It is a double helix (a twisted ladder).
  • A gene is a section of DNA that codes for a particular characteristic (e.g. eye colour) — it is the instructions for making a protein.
  • Sex cells (gametes — sperm and egg) have only 23 chromosomes, one from each pair. At fertilisation they join to make 46 again.
  • Size order, biggest → smallest: nucleus → chromosome → gene → (DNA base).

🔤 Alleles, genotypes & phenotypes

  • An allele is a different version of the same gene (e.g. the gene for flower colour has a red allele and a white allele). You get two alleles for each gene — one from each parent.
  • Dominant allele (capital letter, e.g. R): only one copy is needed for the characteristic to show.
  • Recessive allele (small letter, e.g. r): two copies are needed for the characteristic to show. It is "hidden" if a dominant allele is there.
  • Genotype = the alleles you have (e.g. Rr). Phenotype = the characteristic you can see (e.g. red flowers).
  • Homozygous = two the same (RR or rr). Heterozygous = two different (Rr).
  • A carrier has one copy of a recessive allele for a disorder. They don't have the disorder themselves but can pass the allele to their children.

🧪 Punnett Square Lab — build your own cross

How to draw one: split each parent's two alleles into their gametes (along the top and side), then fill each box with one allele from the top and one from the side. Each box = a 1 in 4 (25%) chance.

🧮 Probability from a Punnett square

  • 4 boxes, so each box is ¼ = 25%. Count the boxes that match what the question asks.
  • Rr × Rr → RR, Rr, Rr, rr → 3 : 1 ratio → 75% show dominant, 25% recessive.
  • RR × rr → all Rr → 100% show the dominant characteristic.
  • Rr × rr → Rr, Rr, rr, rr → 1 : 1 → 50% each.
  • Probability is a chance for each child. A 1 in 4 chance doesn't mean exactly 1 in every 4 children will have it!

🩺 Inherited disorders

Some diseases are caught from other people (infectious). Inherited (genetic) disorders are passed from parents to children in their genes — you can't catch them.

🤔 Look – cover – check

Explain the difference between a dominant and a recessive allele.
A dominant allele only needs one copy for the characteristic to appear. A recessive allele needs two copies — it is only expressed if there is no dominant allele.
Two parents are both carriers of cystic fibrosis (Ff). What is the chance their child has CF?
Punnett square: FF, Ff, Ff, ff. Only ff has CF → 1 in 4 (25%). There's also a 2 in 4 (50%) chance the child is a carrier, and 1 in 4 that they have no faulty allele at all.
Why can't someone be a carrier of polydactyly?
Polydactyly is caused by a dominant allele, so only one copy is needed to have the condition. Anyone with the allele has polydactyly — carriers only exist for recessive disorders.
A red-flowered plant (RR) is crossed with a white-flowered plant (rr). Predict the offspring.
Gametes: R, R and r, r → every box is Rr. All (100%) are red, because R is dominant. They are all heterozygous.
Put in order of size, smallest first: chromosome, nucleus, gene, cell.
gene → chromosome → nucleus → cell

🐄 Breeding & Biotech
Selective breeding · genetic engineering (insulin) · cloning & asexual reproduction

🐕 Selective breeding (artificial selection)

Humans choose which plants or animals breed, to produce organisms with desired characteristics. People have done this for thousands of years — dogs, cats, horses, cattle, wheat.

  1. Choose the desired characteristic (e.g. lots of milk, disease resistance, big fruit, gentle nature).
  2. Select parents that show the characteristic most strongly.
  3. Breed them together.
  4. Select the offspring with the best characteristics and breed them again.
  5. Repeat for many generations until all the offspring show the characteristic.

👍 Advantages

Produces organisms with the characteristics we want (more food, better crops, useful animals). Doesn't need complex technology like genetic engineering.

👎 Disadvantages

Reduces variation (smaller gene pool) → more likely to be wiped out by a new disease, and more inherited disorders from inbreeding. Takes many generations (slow).

💉 Genetic engineering — making human insulin

Genetic engineering = changing an organism's genes by cutting a useful gene out of one organism and putting it into another. The organism that receives it is genetically modified (GM).

  1. The human insulin gene is cut out of human DNA using enzymes ("molecular scissors").
  2. A plasmid (small ring of DNA) is taken from a bacterium and cut open with the same enzyme.
  3. The insulin gene is inserted into the plasmid (stuck in with another enzyme).
  4. The plasmid is put back into a bacterium.
  5. The bacteria reproduce very quickly in a big vat (fermenter) — every copy has the insulin gene.
  6. The bacteria make human insulin, which is collected, purified and given to people with diabetes.

👍 Advantages

Lots of insulin made quickly and cheaply; it's identical to human insulin. GM crops can resist pests/drought or contain extra vitamins (e.g. Golden Rice).

👎 Disadvantages

Worries about long-term effects on health; genes might spread to wild plants; ethical objections ("playing God"); can be expensive to develop.

🌱 Cloning & asexual reproduction

  • Asexual reproduction needs one parent, no gametes and no mixing of genes — the offspring are genetically identical to the parent. Examples: bacteria splitting, yeast budding, strawberry runners, potato tubers.
  • A clone is a genetically identical copy of an organism.
  • Plant cuttings: cut a piece of stem with leaves from a parent plant, dip in rooting powder (plant hormones), plant in damp compost, keep warm and covered. It grows roots → a clone.
  • Tissue culture: a few cells from the parent plant are grown on a jelly with nutrients and hormones → thousands of identical plants.
  • Animal cloning: embryo transplants (split an early embryo, put the parts into surrogate mothers) and adult cell cloning — Dolly the sheep (1996), made by putting the nucleus of a body cell into an egg cell with its nucleus removed.

👍 Advantages

Quick and cheap way to get lots of plants; you know exactly what you'll get (copies of the best plant); can save endangered species.

👎 Disadvantages

No variation → one disease could wipe them all out; cloned animals may have health problems; ethical concerns.

🤔 Look – cover – check

Describe the steps of selective breeding.
Choose the desired characteristic → select parents that show it best → breed them → choose the best offspring and breed them → repeat over many generations.
Give one disadvantage of selective breeding.
It reduces variation in the gene pool, so the organisms are more likely to be killed by a new disease and to suffer inherited disorders. It also takes many generations.
How are bacteria used to make human insulin?
The human insulin gene is cut out with enzymes and inserted into a bacterial plasmid. The plasmid goes back into a bacterium, which reproduces rapidly in a fermenter. The bacteria make insulin, which is purified and used to treat diabetes.
Why are plants grown from cuttings genetically identical to the parent?
Cuttings are a type of asexual reproduction — there's only one parent and no mixing of genes from two gametes, so the new plant has exactly the same genes (a clone).

🍞 Yeast, Fermentation & Enzymes
Fermentation · uses of yeast · enzymes and temperature · investigating fermentation

🦠 Fermentation

Yeast is a single-celled fungus. When it has sugar but no oxygen it does anaerobic respiration, called fermentation:

glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide (+ energy)
  • Bread: yeast ferments the sugars in the dough. The carbon dioxide bubbles make the dough rise. The dough is left somewhere warm so the yeast works faster. The ethanol evaporates when the bread is baked.
  • Beer and wine (brewing): yeast ferments sugars in grain or grapes. Here the ethanol (alcohol) is the useful product; the CO₂ can make it fizzy.
  • Fermentation is controlled by enzymes inside the yeast — so it depends on temperature.

🔑 Enzymes

  • Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts — they speed up chemical reactions without being used up.
  • Each enzyme has an active site with a special shape. Only one substrate fits — the lock and key model.
  • Too cold: molecules move slowly, fewer collisions → slow reaction. The enzyme isn't damaged — warm it up and it works again.
  • Optimum temperature: the temperature where the enzyme works fastest (about 37 °C for human enzymes; yeast works best around 30–40 °C).
  • Too hot: the active site changes shape permanently — the enzyme is denatured and can no longer catalyse the reaction. This can't be undone.

🔬 Investigating fermentation

A typical practical: mix yeast + sugar + water in a test tube or flask, connect a delivery tube into water (or put a balloon on top), and place it in water baths at different temperatures.

  • Independent variable (what you change): temperature.
  • Dependent variable (what you measure): number of CO₂ bubbles per minute (or how much the balloon inflates / volume of gas collected).
  • Control variables (keep the same): amount of yeast, amount and type of sugar, volume of water, time, size of tube.
  • Test for CO₂: bubble it through limewater → it turns cloudy (milky).
  • Typical result: bubbles increase from cold up to about 35–40 °C, then drop quickly as the enzymes denature; at boiling the yeast is killed — no bubbles.

🤔 Look – cover – check

Write the word equation for fermentation.
glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide (+ energy released)
Why does bread dough rise, and why is it left somewhere warm?
Yeast ferments sugars and makes carbon dioxide gas, which gets trapped and makes the dough rise. Warmth speeds up the yeast's enzymes so it ferments faster (but not too hot or they denature).
What happens to an enzyme at a very high temperature?
The shape of its active site changes permanently — it is denatured — so the substrate no longer fits and it can no longer catalyse reactions.
Why does yeast make fewer bubbles at 5 °C than at 35 °C?
At low temperatures the molecules have less energy and move more slowly, so there are fewer collisions between enzymes and substrate — the reaction is slower. The enzymes are not denatured.

🔬 The Scientific Method
Observations · hypotheses · predictions · experiments · results · conclusions

🔁 The steps

  1. Observation — notice something (e.g. "bread rises faster on a warm day").
  2. Question / hypothesis — a possible explanation that can be tested ("yeast ferments faster when it is warmer because its enzymes work faster").
  3. Prediction — what you think will happen, often "the more…, the more…" ("as temperature increases up to 40 °C, the number of bubbles will increase").
  4. Experiment — a fair test: change one variable, measure another, keep everything else the same.
  5. Results — record in a table, repeat and calculate a mean, draw a graph.
  6. Conclusion — describe the pattern and say whether it supports the prediction. Explain it using science.
  7. Evaluation — how could the experiment be improved? Were there any anomalous results?

📊 Variables & key words

  • Independent variable — the one you change (goes on the x-axis / first column of your table).
  • Dependent variable — the one you measure (y-axis).
  • Control variables — everything you keep the same to make it a fair test.
  • Repeatable / reliable — repeat readings give similar results. Repeat and take a mean to improve this.
  • Anomalous result — a result that doesn't fit the pattern. Ignore it when calculating the mean, and repeat that reading.
  • Accurate — close to the true value. Precise — repeat readings are close together.
  • Mean = add the results up ÷ how many there are.

🤔 Look – cover – check

In a yeast experiment testing 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C, name the independent, dependent and one control variable.
Independent: temperature. Dependent: number of CO₂ bubbles per minute. Control: e.g. mass of yeast, mass of sugar, volume of water, time measured for.
What is the difference between a hypothesis and a prediction?
A hypothesis is a possible explanation for an observation. A prediction says what you expect to happen in the experiment if the hypothesis is correct.
Results: 12, 14, 31, 13 bubbles. What's the mean?
31 is anomalous — leave it out. Mean = (12 + 14 + 13) ÷ 3 = 13 bubbles.
Why do scientists repeat experiments?
To check the results are repeatable (reliable), to spot anomalous results, and to calculate a mean.
